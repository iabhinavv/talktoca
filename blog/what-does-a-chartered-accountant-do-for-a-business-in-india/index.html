What a Chartered Accountant actually does for an Indian business

Beyond filing returns: the nine things a CA does across the life of an Indian business, and the standard you should hold the relationship to.

Ask ten Indian business owners what their Chartered Accountant does and most will say some version of "he files my returns". That is accurate and it undersells the role considerably. The filing is the output. The work that determines what goes into it — and what it costs you — happens months earlier.

This article sets out what a CA actually does across the life of an Indian business, in the order you are likely to need it.

Before you start: choosing the structure

The first decision is the entity. A proprietorship is cheap and simple, taxed in the owner's hands at slab rates, with unlimited personal liability. A partnership adds partners and keeps the unlimited liability. An LLP limits liability with a lighter compliance load than a company. A private limited company is the most expensive to maintain — statutory audit every year regardless of turnover — and the only structure institutional investors will realistically fund.

What makes this a CA question rather than a registration-agent question is that each option has different tax consequences, different exit consequences, and different implications for how you can take money out of the business. Converting later is possible but never free.

We have written a fuller comparison in Private Limited vs LLP.

Getting registered — and not over-registered

Beyond incorporation there is PAN and TAN, GST where you cross the threshold or supply inter-state, Udyam registration for MSME benefits, IEC for imports and exports, professional tax in some states, and DPIIT recognition if you qualify as a startup.

The instinct is to get everything. That is a mistake. A GST registration you did not need obliges you to file returns every month for as long as it stays active — including nil returns, with late fees if you miss them. Registrations should be taken deliberately, when they are needed or when the benefit outweighs the ongoing obligation.

Books that survive an assessment

Book-keeping and accounting are not the same thing. Data entry produces a trial balance; accounting produces records that can be defended. The test is whether any number can be traced back to a document that an assessing officer would accept.

In practice that means: invoices in a proper series, input tax credit matched against GSTR-2B rather than assumed, expenses supported by evidence and routed through the banking channel where the law requires it, related-party transactions identified and disclosed, and cash transactions kept within the limits the Income Tax Act prescribes. Businesses that treat this as clerical work pay for it later in disallowed expenses and reversed credit.

The compliance calendar

This is the most visible part of the relationship, and the part most easily judged. A typical private limited company's year includes:

FrequencyObligations
MonthlyGSTR-1 and GSTR-3B, TDS deposit, payroll processing, book-keeping close
QuarterlyTDS returns (24Q / 26Q / 27Q), advance tax instalments in June, September, December and March

None of this is intellectually difficult. All of it carries per-day consequences. The value a CA adds here is not expertise so much as reliability — a calendar that runs without the founder holding it in their head.

Tax planning, properly understood

Legitimate tax planning is unglamorous. It looks like this: comparing the old and new income tax regimes on your actual figures rather than by rule of thumb; structuring director remuneration sensibly between salary, interest and dividend; claiming depreciation and allowances you are entitled to; timing capital expenditure to fall in the right year; deciding whether a presumptive scheme genuinely suits your margins; and characterising each receipt under the correct head of income.

What it does not look like is a scheme whose viability depends on the department not examining it. The distinction matters most three years later, when an assessment is opened and the person who recommended the position has to defend it.

Audit — the underrated part

Companies are audited annually regardless of turnover. Other entities cross into tax audit at prescribed turnover thresholds. Most owners experience this as a cost and a deadline.

It is also the one occasion each year when somebody independent examines how cash, stock and credit actually move through the business. A well-run audit produces a management letter identifying where controls are weak. Reading that letter is optional. It is also where most of the money is.

When the department writes to you

Notices are routine, not catastrophic. The common ones: a mismatch between GSTR-1 and GSTR-3B; a difference between credit claimed and credit available in GSTR-2B; an income tax query where the return does not reconcile with Form 26AS or the Annual Information Statement; a TDS default flagged on TRACES.

Nearly all are answerable with a clean reconciliation and a reply filed inside the window. Proceedings are largely faceless, which means the written submission carries the case. What you want from a CA at this point is a reply drafted properly and filed on time — and an honest read on whether the position is worth defending or worth settling.

Numbers that drive decisions

Once compliance is stable, the same data answers better questions. Which product line makes money after overheads are allocated honestly? How many days of cash remain at the current burn? Why has the working capital cycle stretched by eleven days? Should the next expansion be funded by debt, by equity, or by collecting receivables faster?

This is virtual CFO territory — monthly MIS, forecasting and unit economics. It is the point at which the relationship stops being a cost centre.

Funding, valuation and diligence

When you raise capital, your finance function is what gets examined. Investors run financial and tax due diligence. Valuation reports are needed for pricing and for regulatory filings. Every historic shortcut surfaces in the data room.

Companies with clean books close faster and on better terms. Not because the numbers are larger — because nothing in them requires explaining.

So what should you actually expect?

A reasonable standard for the relationship:

  • You are told what is due before it is due, and asked for documents with time to spare.
  • Filings are confirmed with acknowledgements you can keep.
  • Scope and fees are agreed in writing up front, with government charges shown separately.
  • A qualified professional — not only support staff — reviews your file.
  • You are told when a position is weak, and when a past filing was wrong.
  • Somebody picks up the phone when a notice arrives.

If your current arrangement clears that bar, you are well served. If it does not, the cost of changing is lower than the cost of staying — and it is almost always lower than the accumulated late fees of a calendar nobody is running.

Frequently asked questions

Do I need a CA if my business is small?

Not necessarily for everything — a small proprietorship with simple affairs can manage a lot itself. But even small businesses benefit from a professional at the decision points: choosing the structure, deciding whether to register for GST, choosing between presumptive and regular taxation, and responding to any notice. Those decisions have consequences that outlast the saving on fees.

What is the difference between a CA, a tax consultant and an accountant?

A Chartered Accountant holds a statutory qualification from the Institute of Chartered Accountants of India and is the only one of the three who can sign a statutory audit report or a tax audit report. An accountant or book-keeper maintains records; a tax consultant may prepare and file returns. For anything requiring attestation, certification or representation at a technical level, the qualification matters.

How often should I speak to my CA?

Monthly at minimum if you are GST-registered, because that cycle needs data from you anyway. Beyond routine filing, speak to them before decisions rather than after: taking a new premises in another state, hiring across a threshold, taking a loan, bringing in a partner, or making a large capital purchase. Advice sought before the transaction is worth several times the same advice sought after it.
