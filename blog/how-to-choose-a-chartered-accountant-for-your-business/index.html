How to choose a Chartered Accountant for your business

This is the professional who signs your audit report and defends your tax positions. Six questions worth asking before you engage one.

Most businesses choose their Chartered Accountant on price, a referral, or proximity — and then stay for a decade regardless of how it turns out. Given that this is the professional who signs your audit report and defends your tax positions, it is worth a slightly more deliberate process.

Start with what you actually need

The requirements differ sharply, and a firm that is excellent at one may be indifferent at another:

  • Compliance-only — returns filed accurately and on time. Most small businesses need this and nothing more.
  • Compliance plus advisory — someone who will tell you what a decision costs before you make it.
  • Specialist work — transfer pricing, international taxation, valuation, FEMA, insolvency, litigation before tribunals. These need genuine depth, not general practice.
  • Finance function support — MIS, forecasting, controls. A different skill again, closer to management than to compliance.

Be honest about which of these you need this year. Paying for advisory you never use is as wasteful as buying compliance-only service when you needed advice.

Questions worth asking before you engage

  1. Who will actually do my work, and who reviews it? In many practices the qualified professional signs and articled staff execute. That is normal and fine — but you should know whether a CA has actually reviewed your file or only signed it.
  2. What exactly is inside the annual fee? Get it in writing. Does it include GST returns? TDS returns? ROC filings? Replies to notices? The audit? Quotes that look different are usually covering different scopes.
  3. How are deadlines tracked? Ask what happens two weeks before a due date. A practice that relies on you to remember will cost you late fees eventually.
  4. What happens when a notice arrives? Is drafting a reply included, or billed separately? Will they represent you? This is the moment the relationship is genuinely tested.
  5. Can you give me an example of advice you gave a client that they did not want to hear? The value of a professional is in the position they refuse to sign. A CA who has never had that conversation is not being careful with you either.
  6. How do you handle a handover from my current accountant? A good answer involves reconciling the portals and telling you what is outstanding before taking responsibility.

Signals that should give you pause

  • A fee that is far below everyone else's. Something is out of scope, or the work is going to someone unqualified.
  • Assurance that a refund or a saving is guaranteed before anyone has looked at your records.
  • Reluctance to put scope and fees in writing.
  • Casual suggestions about cash transactions or expenses "we can add".
  • Being unreachable in the two weeks before a major deadline — that is precisely when you will need them.

Does location still matter?

Less than it did. Registrations, returns and audits run through government portals with digital signatures, so a firm in another city can serve you perfectly well. Location matters for two things: physical hearings and inspections, where a locally present firm can attend; and the simple fact that some owners work better face to face.

If you are in the tricity, the practical advantage of a Chandigarh firm is representation — being able to appear before the local income tax and GST authorities when a matter calls for it.

Switching is easier than people assume

Businesses stay in unsatisfactory arrangements because they imagine the handover is painful. In practice it is a document request: the last two to three years of returns, financial statements, GST filings and ROC records, plus portal access. A competent incoming firm reconciles those against the portals, tells you plainly what is outstanding, and prices the clean-up before you commit.

The only genuinely awkward part is the conversation with the outgoing firm, and professional practice has well-established conventions for it. Where a statutory auditor is being changed, there are specific procedures to follow — your incoming firm will handle them.

A reasonable bar. You should be told what is due before it is due, given acknowledgements for every filing, quoted a fixed fee with government charges shown separately, and told plainly when a position is weak. If your current arrangement clears that, stay. If it does not, talk to us.

Frequently asked questions

How do I verify that someone is actually a Chartered Accountant?

Ask for the ICAI membership number and the firm registration number, and verify them through the Institute of Chartered Accountants of India. Only a member in practice can sign a statutory audit report or a tax audit report, so if attestation is part of what you need, verification is worth the two minutes.

Can I change my CA in the middle of a financial year?

Yes. Compliance work can be transferred at any point — the new firm takes over from the next due filing. Where a statutory auditor is being replaced mid-term there are specific procedures under the Companies Act, including intimation to the Registrar, which your incoming firm will handle.

What should a CA's fees include?

Get the scope in writing. A typical annual engagement for a small company covers book-keeping review, GST returns, TDS returns, the income tax return, statutory audit and ROC filings. Notices, representation, registrations and special assignments are commonly billed separately — which is reasonable, provided it is stated up front rather than discovered later.
